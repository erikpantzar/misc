S2 · Recap: define, direct, judge

0.75 min · target end 1:00 · next: S3 The one sentence

Quick recap of last time. Working with AI comes down to three jobs. You define what you want. You direct the AI toward it. And you judge what comes back.

Today is all about the middle one, direct. And the whole thing fits in one idea, which is context.

I'll explain what that word means in a second. For now, hold on to this: when an AI gives you a bad answer, the problem is very often not the AI. It's what you gave it.

S3 · The one sentence

0.5 min · target end 1:30 · next: S4 The desk

If you remember one sentence from this morning, make it this one. The AI is only as good as what it can see.

I'll say it again after every point, so you'll be sick of it by the end. That's the plan.

Everything else today is just different ways of deciding what it can see.

What context is

Everything on the desk is context

4/29

S4 · The desk

1.5 min · target end 3:00 · next: S5 It forgets

So what is context? Context is everything the AI can see right now, in one place. Picture a desk.

On the desk there's a card with the built-in instructions that the tool starts with. There's what I wrote. There are the files it opened. There are results from tools it ran, like a test run or a search. And there's the chat so far, everything we've said to each other.

All of that is the desk. And here's the important part. Nothing else exists for it. If it's not on the desk, the AI doesn't know it. Not your project, not your team, not what you decided last week.

Think of a new colleague who walks in every morning with no memory of yesterday. Smart, fast, willing. But they only know what's on the desk today.

And the desk has a fixed size. The proper name for it is the context window. You'll hear that term a lot, and it just means: how much fits on the desk.

So when you ask yourself why the AI did something dumb, ask a different question first. What was on its desk?

The AI is only as good as what it can see.

What context is

New session, empty desk

5/29

S5 · It forgets

0.75 min · target end 3:45 · next: S6 The desk fills up

Next, the part people find surprising. It forgets.

Start a new session and the desk is empty. Yesterday's chat is gone, unless it was written down somewhere the AI reads at the start. That's it. That's the whole rule.

So whenever you hear yourself say "but it knew this yesterday", that is never true. Yesterday someone put it on the desk. Maybe you, maybe a file, maybe a setting. Today nobody did.

And that's good news, because it means you're in control. If you want it to know something tomorrow, you make sure it's on tomorrow's desk.

Keep that in mind, because most of the eight things later are about exactly that.

The desk fills up

6/29

S6 · The desk fills up

1 min · target end 4:45 · next: S7 How big is the desk

Now the desk also fills up. Everything is counted in tokens, which are roughly word pieces. Every file it opens, every answer it writes, every tool result takes up room.

Here's a bar showing it. Instructions, my messages, files, tool results, and old chat. It doesn't take long before the old chat and the tool results are the biggest part, and that's usually the part you care about least.

And a full desk has a cost. Things get slower, and more expensive, and the answers tend to get worse. The old, off-topic stuff competes with what actually matters right now.

A long chat with an AI is a desk covered in old paper.

The AI is only as good as what it can see, and it can see worse when the desk is full.

Bigger desk, not a cleaner desk

7/29

S7 · How big is the desk

0.75 min · target end 5:30 · next: S8 Two ways to fail

So how big is the desk? This changes every few months, so these are today's numbers.

Claude, GPT-6 and Gemini 4 all list about a million tokens. That's roughly five hundred and fifty thousand words, about a small library shelf. The answer it can write back is capped much lower, around 128 thousand tokens.

Open models vary a lot and I don't have solid numbers, so I left them off.

Now the thing to take away. A bigger desk is not a cleaner desk. A million tokens of mess is still a mess. The size gives you room, it doesn't give you focus. Focus is your job.

What context is

Too little. Too much.

8/29

S8 · Two ways to fail

1.25 min · target end 6:45 · next: S9 Eight things you can do

So there are two ways to get this wrong.

The first is too little. You write one line, the desk is nearly empty, and the AI fills the gaps with a safe, generic guess. It's not being lazy. It simply can't see what you meant. Most bad answers are this one.

A small example. You tell it "make the button blue" and it makes every button in the app blue. It wasn't wrong. It just couldn't see which button you meant.

The second is too much. You paste everything, the chat runs for hours, there are old instructions in there that no longer apply. Now it gets lost, or it follows something you said three hours ago that you've already changed your mind about. It's like a colleague handed a thousand pages and told "it's in there somewhere".

So the job is simple to say, and a bit hard to do. Put the right things on the desk, and nothing else.

That brings us to the practical part. Eight things you can do.

10/29

S10 · The four-part ask

0.75 min · target end 8:00 · next: S11 One line vs four parts

Number one. Give it a real ask. A real ask has four parts.

The goal: what does done look like? Without it, the AI picks its own finish line.

The context: what can't it see? Point at the files, the design, the stack. If it's not on the desk, it doesn't exist.

The limits: what must it not do? No new libraries, don't touch other code, no extra features. Fences stop guessing.

And the check: how will it prove it works? Otherwise "done" just means "it looks right to me".

Goal, context, limits, check.

S11 · One line vs four parts

1.25 min · target end 9:15 · next: S12 Plan first

Here's the same task, asked two ways. The example is a small movie site of mine called emdb, and the task is to add a watchlist.

On the left, one line: add a watchlist to emdb. The AI has to guess where to store things, what a watchlist even looks like, whether to add a library, and which pages to touch. So you get a generic answer that you then spend an hour correcting.

On the right, the four-part version. A bookmark button, a link in the header with a count, a watchlist page. Use what's already there. No new libraries, store it in the browser. And run the build and compare screenshots before and after.

To be clear, both of these are sketches of what to expect. I'm not running anything live this morning, and I'm not claiming these exact results. The point is the difference in what the AI had to guess.

And look at the last part, the check. It's what lets you look at the result and not take "done" at its word.

12/29

S12 · Plan first

1.25 min · target end 10:30 · next: S9a Ask and plan, skim

Still on number one, because this is the cheapest trick I know. Plan first.

In Claude Code there's a plan mode. You give it the same four-part ask, and it reads the code and proposes a plan, but it changes nothing until you say yes.

Why does that matter? Because the cheapest place to catch a wrong assumption is before any code exists. The plan might say, one, a hook that stores the watchlist. Two, a button on each card. Three, a watchlist page. And then a question: store only ids, or ids plus title and poster?

I answer that in ten seconds. If it had built the wrong version first, that same correction would be a rewrite.

Say what done looks like, and agree the plan before any code. The AI is only as good as what it can see, and a plan shows you what it sees.

CLAUDE.md: the rules file

14/29

S13 · CLAUDE.md

1.25 min · target end 11:45 · next: S9b CLAUDE.md, skim

Number two. A file called CLAUDE.md. It's a short text file in your project that the AI reads at the start of every session. It's what you'd tell a new colleague on day one.

What goes in it: how we build, how to run things, what to avoid. For emdb that's the stack, the commands, the style, and a rule: no new dependencies without asking. Everything in this example comes from the repo. The wording is illustrative, because emdb doesn't have one yet.

You can have layers. A personal one for everything you do, and one per project.

Here's the catch. Every line is read in every session. So every line takes desk space, every time. A good rule: if removing a line wouldn't change the AI's behaviour, it goes.

Keep it short, and only lines that change behaviour.

Check emdb has no CLAUDE.md today. Say "this is the one I'd add", not "this is the one in the repo".

3 · Memory

If it matters tomorrow, write it down

16/29

S14 · Memory: write it down

1.25 min · target end 13:00 · next: S9c Memory, skim

Number three. Memory, which really just means: write it down.

Remember, it forgets between sessions. So anything worth keeping has to live in a file that the next session reads. Two kinds. The rules go in CLAUDE.md. The facts and decisions go in notes, or a plan file.

A simple version: keep the plan and the decisions in a notes file in the project. When you start a session tomorrow you say: read this note and continue from the open items. And it starts where you stopped, instead of asking what the project is.

Claude Code can also save memories by itself in the background. Decide whether you want that. Some people prefer to write things down on purpose, so what's remembered is what they chose and they can read it. Try both.

The trap is something that lives only in the chat. Then one clear, or one new day, and it's gone.

If it matters tomorrow, it can't live only in the chat.

4 · MCP

MCP: plug in sources

18/29

S15 · MCP

1.5 min · target end 14:30 · next: S9d MCP, skim

Number four. MCP, which stands for Model Context Protocol. Forget the name. It's a standard plug, so the AI can use your tools and data directly, instead of you copying and pasting from them.

Here's how I explain it to developers. On the left, a normal web API. It's for a program that already knows the address. A developer writes code that calls it. On the right, the same thing as an MCP tool. It has a name, a description, and a list of inputs. The AI reads that description, picks the tool, and fills in the inputs.

So it's the same thing underneath. MCP just adds a self-description that the AI can read.

Think of tickets. Without it, you open the ticket, copy the text, paste it in. With a ticket tool plugged in, you say: look up this ticket and tell me what's needed. And it fetches it itself.

One warning. Every tool you plug in puts a description on the desk. Plug in everything just in case, and you get a big noisy desk.

Connect it to the source instead of pasting from it, and only what the task needs.

5 · Skills

Skills: instructions that load on demand

20/29

S16 · Skills

1.25 min · target end 15:45 · next: S9e Skills, skim

Number five. Skills. A skill is a saved how-to in a folder. How I review a pull request. How I write a note into my notes folder.

The clever part is that only the short description sits on the desk. The full steps load only when the job needs them. That's the difference from the rules file. CLAUDE.md is always on. A skill is on demand.

Say you have one called commit-helper. Its description says: split pending changes into suitable commits, and write messages in this repo's style. You say, commit my changes. It matches the description, loads the steps, and follows them.

A skill costs almost nothing until it's used, so it can be long.

The trap is a vague description. Then it never triggers, or it triggers at the wrong time.

Rules that apply every time go in CLAUDE.md. How-tos for one job go in a skill.

6 · Agents

Delegate the digging, keep the deciding

22/29

S17 · Agents

1.25 min · target end 17:00 · next: S9f Agents, skim

Number six. Agents, and delegating. A helper agent gets its own clean desk. You give it a brief, it does the digging or the job, and it comes back with a short summary. Your desk only receives the summary.

Be honest about what that buys you. Delegating doesn't make the answer better by itself, and it costs more tokens. The good reasons are three: work that can run in parallel, noisy output you don't want on your desk, and a fresh reviewer who hasn't seen your reasoning. Bad: a vague job with no check, or a small edit that's faster to do yourself.

Two rules. The helper knows nothing except what you write, so give it a full brief, the same four parts as before. And give it a check it can't skip. In my experience helpers over-report: they say a job is done when it's mostly done. So have a different, fresh helper review the work.

Delegate the digging, keep the deciding.

24/29

S18 · Clear and compact

1.25 min · target end 18:15 · next: S9g Clear and compact, skim

Number seven. Clearing and compacting. Two ways to free the desk.

Slash clear empties it completely. Slash compact summarises the chat so far and keeps the summary. And you can tell compact what to keep: the files touched, the test status, the open decisions, and the unresolved questions.

How I use them. New task: make sure the plan and decisions are written down, then clear. Nothing is lost, because it's written down. Same task, getting long: compact.

Signs the desk is full: it repeats itself, forgets a rule you gave it, drifts off the task. Don't argue with it. Write a handoff note and start fresh.

One trap. Compacting a chat that's already confused just keeps the confusion. A fresh session with a good note beats a long compacted one.

You can also see the desk. Slash context shows how full it is and what is taking the space.

Fresh desk, one job.

8 · Workflows

Every step starts clean

26/29

S19 · Workflows

1.5 min · target end 19:45 · next: S9h Workflows, skim

Number eight, which ties it together. A workflow is the steps you repeat, written down. Plan. Build. Review. Check. And the key idea: each step starts on a fresh desk, and hands over files, not chat.

The smallest version, for anyone: plan in plan mode and approve. Build in a fresh session that reads the plan file. Review in another fresh session, with the check from the beginning.

Once you've done it by hand a few times, wrap the repeated steps in a skill or a command, so you don't explain them every time. That's number five and number six working together.

A bigger version does this from a ticket to a draft pull request, with you approving at one stop. Notice how much of it is deciding and checking, and how little is typing.

The trap is automating a step you haven't done by hand a few times.

Every step starts clean, and reads what the last step wrote down.
